## Introduction
### Walden
>[!quote] "The mass of men lead lives of quiet desperation. What is called resignation is confirmed desperation. From the desperate city you go into the desperate country, and have to console yourself with the bravery of minks and muskrats. "
>\- Henry David Thoreau *[[Walden]]*, 1854 

Thoreau writes of his neighbors, toiling away in their farms, houses and properties, *"The mass of men lead lives of quiet desperation."* Thoreau, rather literally, argues that the causes of this desperation are systems of society that set expectations of how to live (he was a [[Transcendentalism|transcendentalist]] after all, so this conclusion is not surprising). Societal expectations of salary, family structure, recreation, etc. dominated the common person's mind. How and why every activity "ought" to be done was the typical discourse. Thoreau instead considers what is the *"chief end of man"*, and what are *"the true necessaries and means of life."* The result of this consideration was *Walden*, in which Thoreau describes in detail how he *"went to the woods because [he] wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if [he] could not learn what it had to teach."* Thoreau stresses the importance of deliberate choice in all facets of life, and criticizes the *"common mode of living"*, suggesting that those who follow such doctrines *"think there is no choice left."* Thoreau believes that there is a choice, and that *"no way of thinking or doing, however ancient, can be trusted without proof."* 

Thoreau's words have resonated across generations. American poet Robert Frost wrote of Walden, *"In one book [...] he surpasses everything we have had in America".* *Walden* has earned its place in [[Transcendentalism|transcendental philosophy]] and American literature classics, but the famous *Walden* line *"The mass of men lead lives of quiet desperation"* is left under-explored.

### Stoner
*"Mr. Shakespeare speaks to you across three hundred years, Mr. Stoner; do you hear him? [...] What does he say to you, Mr. Stoner? What does his sonnet mean?"* The words of the English Literature professor Archer Sloane echo through the speechless young mind of William Stoner. At that moment, a fire had been ignited. 

*Stoner* by John Williams tells the story of William Stoner, who leads a life of quiet desperation in a different way than Thoreau considered. The novel serves to grapple with this quiet desperation, to give the reader a view into the unspoken, invisible world in which one man's interior experience toils over the minutiae of his responsibilities. Zach Woods's contemporary description of internal experience resonates with *Stoner*'s themes, illustrating the puzzle Williams faces with *Stoner*; we are not accustomed to understanding others at such a personal level.

>[!quote] [My] interior experience has at times been so full of strange horror [...] And because of that, now, I operate under the assumption that everyone has an inner life that may not bear much of a resemblance to their outer life [...] *People have torments and glories that are happening inside, completely out of view, but that might actually be the core drama of their entire life.*
>\- Zach Woods *Take Your Shoes Off* Episode 258, 2024

While written in third person, John Williams tells this story from an intensely personal and vulnerable perspective, capturing the small moments of discomfort, disappointment, torments, and triumphs that Stoner feels as his life passes him by. Thoreau's original material purpose of "quiet desperation" can be reinterpreted as an emotional one, and in *Stoner*, Williams captures and shows plainly to the reader as one would experience their own life, the internal experiences of Stoner's mind and the "quiet desperation" found within. 


### Discovery
William Stoner was born in 1891 on a small farm in Missouri. His childhood was quiet and laborious. Milking cows, gathering eggs, and attending school. When his father suggested he attend the new College of Agriculture at the University in Columbia, it took him by surprise, but he agreed nonetheless. In the first semester of his second year at the University, he took a required course on English literature taught by Archer Sloane. Stoner struggled with the course. His approach to his other schoolwork followed his approach to his farm duties growing up: steadfast but indifferent. Indifferent as a farmer must be to the rain to keep their sanity. This approach yielded good results for his more empirical classes, but fell short in literature. It became clear to Stoner that there was something different about this course when professor Sloane *"erupted into anger"*, demanding to know if Stoner understood Shakespeare's words. Stoner did not, but he did finally understand the weight of those words flowing through Sloane as he recited the sonnet from memory. 

John Williams delicately describes the moments following this confrontation almost entirely from the perspective of an outside observer. Only in the final sentence of this excerpt does Williams reveal directly what Stoner "felt", allowing the reader to peer over the wall of Stoner's interior experience. We watch Stoner from afar exit the classroom. We slowly get closer as his classmates brush past him to get to their classes. And finally we peer over the wall and see his bewilderment when he *"felt very distant from them and very close to them."*

>[!quote] William Stoner was hardly aware of the students about him who rose grumbling and muttering from their seats and shuffled out of the room. For several minutes after they left he sat unmoving, staring out before him at the narrow planked flooring that had been worn bare of varnish by the restless feet of the students he would never see or know. He slid his own feet across the floor, hearing the dry rasp of wood on his soles, and feeling the roughness through the leather. Then he too got up and went slowly out of the room. </br> </br>The thin chill of the late fall day cut through his clothing. He looked around him, at the bare gnarled branches of the trees that curled and twisted against the pale sky. Students, hurrying across the campus to their classes, brushed against him; he heard the mutter of their voices and the click of their heels upon the stone paths, and saw their faces, flushed by the cold, bend downward against a slight breeze. He looked at them curiously, as if he had not seen them before, and felt very distant from them and very close to them.

What Williams conveys is a complex torrent of thoughts and feelings that can only be understood empathically. The literal words printed on the page are of course pretty and illustrative, but the annihilation and revelation and utter confusion that is evoked are akin to a firsthand account of ego death, or a miracle. 

>[!note] Personal aside
>There may be no way to describe why this passage evokes such a powerful emotional response for me. When I read this passage, I see Stoner, and I see myself. I have felt what I believe Stoner feels after walking out of that classroom. I have felt what I believe Sloane feels when he desperately tries to convince Stoner there is something worthwhile in Shakespeare's words, because right now, I am desperate to convince you that there is something worthwhile about this moment.
>
>Stoner will later struggle to explain why this moment had such a tremendous impact on his own life, and I think neither he nor I can do it justice.

### Notes to self
- Desperate, frantic, quiet indifference → [[Cognitive Dissonance]]
- "Quiet desperation" in many ways was Thoreau's problem statement, and the rest of Walden serves as his solution. By recontextualizing the problem as emotional, rather than a material lacking, how does that affect the solutions Thoreau suggests?
- Dharma/meaning of life conversation in relation to Sloanes first outburst in Stoner.
- Character complexity in depth, while lacking dramatic substance.
- Stoner is an observer in his own story, except when he's not. Small moments of pitiful bravery. Sometimes Stoner has no choice but to do the thing he must do. 
- Stoner and the quiet desperation of sharing your innermost passions. 
- Good quote:
>[!quote] I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if I could not learn what it had to teach, and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived. I did not wish to live what was not life, living is so dear; nor did I wish to practise resignation, unless it was quite necessary.
>\- Henry David Thoreau *[[Walden]]*, 1854
